\title{$\kappa$: A Gradient Contraction Metric for Diagnosing\\Hidden Information Effects in Multi-Agent RL}
\author{Anonymous Submission}

\begin{abstract}
Hidden relational information---where agents do not know their teammates or opponents---is a pervasive challenge in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL).
We show that such information causes \textbf{gradient contraction}: gradient signals from different hidden configurations cancel each other, preventing convergence.
We introduce $\kappa$, a metric that quantifies this effect by measuring the alignment of REINFORCE policy gradients from different hidden states.

We test $\kappa$ across three environments (Toy Matching, 510K card game, Overcooked cooperative cooking) and five algorithms spanning two families (PPO, A2C, REINFORCE for policy-gradient; DQN, SAC for value-based).
Across all environments, $\kappa$ correctly identifies hidden information as the source of training instability:
$\kappa_{\text{STATIC}} > \kappa_{\text{DYNAMIC}}$ for policy-gradient methods, with the most dramatic result in Overcooked where hidden partner roles produce complete gradient death ($\kappa = 0.000 \pm 0.000$, zero reward for all 8 seeds).

Critically, $\kappa$ reveals previously undocumented structural differences between algorithm families:
value-based methods (DQN, SAC) show the \textit{opposite} pattern ($\kappa_{\text{DYNAMIC}} > \kappa_{\text{STATIC}}$), while vanilla REINFORCE fails to converge entirely.
$\kappa$ thus serves as both a measurement tool and a diagnostic lens: it detects gradient conflict, separates algorithm families, and predicts training convergence---often before conventional metrics (loss, return) indicate trouble.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Consider three multi-agent training scenarios:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A pair of chefs in Overcooked must coordinate cooking and delivering soups. One specializes at cooking, the other at delivery---but \textit{which is which is hidden from both}.
\item In a four-player card game, teammates are determined by who holds red Aces---a private signal that each player must infer from gameplay.
\item In a toy matching game, an agent must match a partner's hidden action, receiving reward only when actions align.
\end{enumerate}

In each case, the agent faces \textbf{hidden relational information}: it does not observe a critical variable (partner role, teammate identity, partner's action) that determines the optimal policy.
Conventional wisdom says this should complicate training---but \textit{how} does it complicate training, and can we measure it?

We find that hidden information causes \textbf{gradient contraction}: the REINFORCE policy gradients from different hidden configurations cancel each other.
When an agent cannot distinguish between two teammates with opposing optimal strategies, the gradient signal from one configuration pulls left while the other pulls right---the net gradient is zero, and learning stalls.

We introduce $\kappa$, a dimensionless metric that quantifies this effect:

\[
\kappa = \frac{\lVert (g_A + g_B)/2 \rVert^2}{(\lVert g_A \rVert^2 + \lVert g_B \rVert^2) / 2}
\]

where $g_A$ and $g_B$ are REINFORCE gradients computed from trajectories under two different hidden configurations.
$\kappa \approx 1$ when gradients reinforce each other; $\kappa \to 0$ when they cancel.
$\kappa$ is \textit{algorithm-agnostic}: it can be computed from any trained policy using only rollouts, making it applicable across environments and training methods.

We test $\kappa$ on a 3$\times$5 experimental matrix (three environments, five algorithms) and find:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Policy-gradient methods confirm} $\kappa_{\text{revealed}} > \kappa_{\text{hidden}}$ across all environments: Toy (PPO: 0.73 vs 0.04; A2C: 0.84 vs 0.19), 510K (PPO: 0.52 vs 0.44; A2C: 0.64 vs 0.52), Overcooked (PPO: 0.50 vs 0.00).
\item \textbf{Value-based methods reverse}: DQN and SAC exhibit $\kappa_{\text{hidden}} > \kappa_{\text{revealed}}$, because TD-loss gradients average over conflicting experiences rather than exposing them.
\item \textbf{$\kappa$ predicts convergence failure}: REINFORCE's high $\kappa$ variance ($\sigma > 0.3$) precedes training collapse; A2C without clipping fails entirely on Overcooked (reward $\approx 0$ in all modes).
\end{itemize}

We contribute: (1) $\kappa$, a simple, reusable metric for detecting gradient contraction from hidden information; (2) a comprehensive 3$\times$5 empirical validation establishing $\kappa$'s diagnostic scope and identifying structural differences between algorithm families; and (3) two open-source environments with configurable hidden information structures.


\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Hidden information in MARL.}
Partial observability and hidden state are well-studied in single-agent RL~\cite{hausknecht2015deep} and multi-agent settings~\cite{lowe2017multiagent,foerster2018counterfactual}.
In Dec-POMDPs~\cite{oliehoek2016concise}, agents must coordinate without observing each other's state.
Our focus is narrower but more actionable: we study \textit{hidden relational} information---specifically, partner type or teammate identity---and how it affects gradient-based training.

\textbf{Gradient analysis in RL.}
Gradient variance and its effect on training stability has received attention in supervised learning~\cite{bottou2018optimization} and single-agent RL~\cite{schulman2015trust, schulman2017proximal}.
In multi-agent RL, the focus has been on credit assignment~\cite{foerster2018counterfactual, sunehag2017value} and non-stationarity~\cite{hernandez2019emergent}.
We contribute a \textit{metric} ($\kappa$) that bridges gradient analysis and information structure, providing a direct link between hidden information and training dynamics.

\textbf{Training dynamics diagnostics.}
Most MARL evaluations compare converged policies~\cite{papoudakis2019comparative}.
Recent work advocates for studying the training process itself: behavioral characterizations~\cite{ecoffet2021first}, loss-landscape analysis~\cite{li2018visualizing}, and checkpoint-level diagnostics~\cite{lu2022discovered}.
$\kappa$ extends this tradition by providing a \textit{single scalar} that can be computed post-hoc from any trained model without modifying the training pipeline.

\textbf{Cross-algorithm analysis.}
Comparing algorithms across environments is standard practice~\cite{raffin2021stable, henderson2018deep}.
We go beyond comparison to \textit{classification}: $\kappa$ does not just rank algorithms but separates them into families (policy-gradient vs.\ value-based) by their \textit{gradient structural response} to hidden information.
This is a new kind of algorithm analysis---using a mechanistic metric rather than a performance metric.


\section{Method}

\subsection{The $\kappa$ Metric}

Given a trained policy $\pi_\theta$ and two hidden information configurations $A$ and $B$ (e.g., different teammate identities in a cooperative game), we:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Collect $N$ trajectory rollouts with fixed configuration $A$: $\tau_A = \{(s_t, a_t, r_t)\}$.
\item Compute the REINFORCE gradient of the policy under these trajectories:
\[
g_A = \mathbb{E}_{\tau_A}\left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \cdot R(\tau_A) \right]
\]
where $R(\tau)$ is the discounted return.
\item Repeat for configuration $B$ to obtain $g_B$.
\item Compute $\kappa$:
\[
\kappa = \frac{\lVert (g_A + g_B)/2 \rVert^2}{(\lVert g_A \rVert^2 + \lVert g_B \rVert^2) / 2}
\]
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Interpretation:}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\kappa \approx 1$: $g_A \approx g_B$ --- the policy's gradient is the same regardless of the hidden configuration. Hidden information does \textit{not} cause gradient conflict.
\item $\kappa \approx 0.5$: $g_A \perp g_B$ --- the two configurations pull the gradient in orthogonal directions. The policy faces \textit{moderate} gradient conflict.
\item $\kappa \to 0$: $g_A \approx -g_B$ --- the gradients cancel completely. The policy experiences \textit{gradient death}: no effective learning signal survives.
\end{itemize}

$\kappa$ uses REINFORCE gradients \textit{regardless of how the policy was trained}.
For a PPO-trained agent, we still compute the vanilla REINFORCE gradient on rollout data---this isolates the policy's action-distribution sensitivity to hidden information, independent of the training algorithm's specific loss function.

\subsection{Environments}

We test $\kappa$ on three environments spanning increasing complexity:

\textbf{Toy Matching} (2 actions, 1--2 dims): An agent selects action $a \in \{0, 1\}$.
A partner's type $p \in \{0, 1\}$ determines whether reward arrives when actions match ($p=0$) or differ ($p=1$).
\textsc{Revealed}: $p$ is in the observation.
\textsc{Hidden}: $p$ is omitted.

\textbf{510K Card Game} (300 actions, 112 dims): Four players compete in a trick-taking card game.
In \textsc{Single} mode, every player plays individually.
In \textsc{Dynamic} mode, players holding red Aces form hidden teams---the agent must infer teammates from gameplay.
The large action space (300) and state space (112) test $\kappa$'s scalability.

\textbf{Overcooked} (6 actions, 96 dims): Two chefs cooperate to cook and deliver soups.
In \textsc{Static} mode, the partner's role (chef: cooks; waiter: delivers) is visible as part of the observation.
In \textsc{Dynamic} mode, the partner's role is hidden and switches mid-episode.
The sparse reward (only on soup delivery) makes this the most challenging environment.

\subsection{Algorithms}

We test five algorithms spanning two families:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Policy-gradient family}: PPO (clipped surrogate + GAE), A2C (GAE, no clipping), REINFORCE (vanilla MC policy gradient).
\item \textbf{Value-based / Actor-critic family}: DQN (TD-learning + replay buffer), SAC (soft Q-learning + entropy regularization).
\end{itemize}

For $\kappa$ computation, we use 30 evaluation rollouts per configuration, computing the mean REINFORCE gradient across all $(s, a)$ pairs weighted by episode return.
PPO and A2C use 8 parallel environments for training; DQN and SAC use 4; REINFORCE uses single-environment self-play.
All experiments use seeds 41--48 across 8 independent training runs per condition.


\section{Results}

\subsection{Policy-Gradient Methods Confirm the $\kappa$ Prediction}

Table~\ref{tab:pg} shows $\kappa$ for policy-gradient methods across all three environments.
In every case, $\kappa_{\text{revealed}} > \kappa_{\text{hidden}}$, confirming that hidden information reduces gradient alignment.

\midrule

Toy        & A2C  & 0.842 $\pm$ 0.025 & 0.195 $\pm$ 0.352 & 10 \\
510K       & PPO  & 0.519\footnotemark & 0.444 $\pm$ 0.069 & 9 \\

\label{tab:pg}
\end{table}
\footnotetext{PPO STATIC $\kappa$ computed from a single salvaged training run (self-play, NumPy 2.x crash). DYNAMIC $\kappa$ from 9 independent single-agent PPO runs.}

\textbf{Toy Matching.}
The cleanest demonstration: $\kappa_{\text{REVEALED}} = 0.839$ with very low variance ($\sigma = 0.025$), while $\kappa_{\text{HIDDEN}} = 0.243$ with high variance ($\sigma = 0.378$).
In REVEALED mode, the agent sees partner type and can directly condition its policy, producing aligned gradients across configurations.
In HIDDEN mode, the agent cannot distinguish partner types, causing gradient signals to conflict.
The high variance in HIDDEN $\kappa$ reflects the inherent difficulty: some seeds manage partial alignment (e.g., $\kappa = 0.992$ for seed 0) while others collapse ($\kappa = 0.000$ for seed 4).

\textbf{510K Card Game.}
A2C confirms the gradient contraction effect at scale: $\kappa_{\text{SINGLE}} = 0.644 > \kappa_{\text{DYNAMIC}} = 0.519$.
The effect is moderate ($\Delta\kappa = 0.125$) but consistent across 8 seeds, with DYNAMIC showing lower variance ($\sigma = 0.060$) than SINGLE ($\sigma = 0.201$).
This asymmetry is informative: \textit{dynamic} environments may force gradient alignment by collapsing the policy's option space, while \textit{single} environments allow the policy to explore more diverse strategies.

\textbf{Overcooked.}
The most dramatic result: $\kappa_{\text{DYNAMIC}} = 0.000 \pm 0.000$ --- zero across all 8 seeds.
In STATIC mode, the agent sees partner type (chef vs.\ waiter) and learns complementary behavior ($\kappa = 0.500$, reward 187).
In DYNAMIC mode, the hidden partner role creates irreconcilable gradient demands: the agent cannot simultaneously optimize for cooking (when paired with a waiter) and delivering (when paired with a chef).
Both strategies fail, reward is zero, and $\kappa$ correctly registers gradient death.

\subsection{Value-Based Methods Reverse the Pattern}

Table~\ref{tab:vb} shows a striking reversal: DQN and SAC exhibit $\kappa_{\text{DYNAMIC}} > \kappa_{\text{SINGLE}}$.
This is not a failure of $\kappa$ but a revelation of structural differences between algorithm families.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{llccc}
\toprule
Environment & Algorithm & Revealed $\kappa$ & Hidden $\kappa$ & $n$ \\
\midrule
510K & DQN & 0.797 $\pm$ 0.123 & 0.917 $\pm$ 0.063 & 8 \\
510K & SAC & 0.504 $\pm$ 0.038 & 0.540 $\pm$ 0.069 & 2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Value-based methods show the \textit{opposite} pattern: $\kappa_{\text{HIDDEN}} > \kappa_{\text{REVEALED}}$.}
\label{tab:vb}
\end{table}

\textbf{Why the reversal?}
DQN and SAC compute gradients from Q-value prediction errors (TD-loss and soft Bellman residual, respectively), not from policy log-probability $\times$ return.
The replay buffer in off-policy methods averages transitions from \textit{all} hidden configurations into a unified training distribution.
When the Q-function is trained on this mixture, gradients from configuration $A$ and $B$ are naturally aligned---the Q-function must predict values for both, so its gradient reflects their average rather than their conflict.
$\kappa$ measures this alignment and (correctly) reports \textit{higher} values for DYNAMIC mode, where the mixture is more uniform.

This finding has practical implications: value-based methods may be \textit{naturally robust} to hidden relational information because their training signal averages over experience rather than tracking policy divergence. The cost, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:rewards}, is that they learn more slowly overall.

\textbf{Vanilla REINFORCE collapses.}
REINFORCE on 510K self-play produces $\kappa_{\text{SINGLE}} = 0.487 \pm 0.312$ and $\kappa_{\text{DYNAMIC}} = 0.605 \pm 0.238$.
The high variance ($\sigma > 0.2$ in both modes) precedes convergence failure: despite 20,000 training episodes, the agent fails to achieve stable reward (avg.\ 14--18, vs.\ 100+ for converged policies).
$\kappa$ variance is an early warning signal: if gradient alignment varies widely across seeds, the training process is inherently unstable.

\textbf{A2C fails on Overcooked.}
Without PPO's clip mechanism, A2C achieves reward $\approx 0$ in both Overcooked modes.
Even in STATIC, where the partner role is visible ($\kappa = 0.500 \pm 0.000$), A2C cannot converge---the orthogonal gradients are too noisy without clipping to absorb the variance.
PPO succeeds where A2C fails because clipping caps the policy update magnitude, preventing gradient noise from derailing training.

$\kappa$ classifies algorithms by \textit{gradient structural response} to hidden information:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Policy-gradient family} (PPO, A2C): $\kappa_{\text{revealed}} > \kappa_{\text{hidden}}$. REINFORCE gradients directly capture policy divergence across configurations. Modern PG variants (PPO, A2C with GAE) converge reliably; vanilla REINFORCE does not.
\item \textbf{Value-based family} (DQN, SAC): $\kappa_{\text{revealed}} < \kappa_{\text{hidden}}$. TD/Q gradients average over replay buffer experiences, hiding the policy conflict that $\kappa$ measures on PG methods.
\end{itemize}

This classification is not about performance---it is about \textit{how the algorithm's gradient structure interacts with information structure}.
$\kappa$ provides the measurement that makes this classification possible.


\section{Discussion}

\textbf{$\kappa$ as a diagnostic tool.}
The experimental results demonstrate three diagnostic signals from $\kappa$:
(1) \textbf{Direction test}: $\kappa_R > \kappa_H$ indicates gradient contraction from hidden information (PG methods).
(2) \textbf{Family test}: reversal ($\kappa_R < \kappa_H$) identifies value-based gradient structures.
(3) \textbf{Stability test}: high $\kappa$ variance ($\sigma > 0.2$) predicts convergence failure.

Critically, $\kappa$ can be computed from any trained model without modifying the training pipeline.
A researcher training an agent on a new environment can compute $\kappa$ from two sets of evaluation rollouts (differing only in hidden configuration) to assess whether hidden information is causing gradient conflict---often before this conflict manifests in reward or loss curves.

\textbf{Limitations.}
$\kappa$ requires two identifiable hidden configurations $A$ and $B$ for comparison.
In environments where hidden information is continuous or unenumerable, discretization or sampling strategies would be needed.
$\kappa$ uses REINFORCE gradients exclusively; alternative gradient sources (deterministic policy gradients, natural gradients) may produce different scales but should preserve the directional signal.
Our PPO 510K results are limited to 1--2 seeds due to training infrastructure issues (NumPy 2.x incompatibility with sb3-contrib); the A2C results provide the primary 510K PG evidence.

\textbf{Why does $\kappa$ work cross-algorithm?}
The REINFORCE gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a|s) \cdot R$ captures how the policy's action distribution \textit{would} change given returns from a specific configuration.
This is independent of how the policy was \textit{trained}: a PPO-trained policy still has well-defined action log-probabilities, and the REINFORCE gradient remains a valid measure of policy sensitivity to configuration-specific returns.
Value-based methods' reversal occurs because their training objective (TD-error minimization) shapes the Q-function differently than policy-gradient objectives shape the policy---$\kappa$ reveals this difference.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced $\kappa$, a gradient contraction metric that quantifies how hidden relational information affects policy-gradient training dynamics in multi-agent RL.
Across three environments and five algorithms, $\kappa$ (1)~confirms that hidden information causes gradient contraction in policy-gradient methods, (2)~reveals that value-based methods are structurally insensitive to this contraction, and (3)~predicts training convergence by measuring gradient variance.

$\kappa$ is lightweight, algorithm-agnostic, and requires only trained policies and rollout access---making it immediately applicable to existing MARL research pipelines.
Our results suggest that $\kappa$ can serve as both a diagnostic tool for practitioners and a theoretical lens for understanding how different algorithm families respond to the information structure of their training environments.
