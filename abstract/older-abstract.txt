Short learning trajectories are often interpreted as evidence of stable optimization and policy convergence. We show why this inference can fail in partially observable multi-agent reinforcement learning. When agents cannot observe relational variables such as teammate identity, role, or task assignment, policy gradients that are useful within individual relational contexts are projected onto a coarser local information space and may cancel. The policy can therefore move little not because it has converged, but because it lacks the information required to improve. We formalize this failure mode as Information-Induced Gradient Contraction, derive a Pythagorean decomposition of relation-conditioned gradient energy, and introduce the Relational Gradient Retention Ratio, κ. Combined with a drift–diffusion analysis, κ provides a Stable-or-Stuck diagnostic that separates low-noise convergence from information-induced stagnation. An analytic latent-relation coordination problem exhibits the extreme case: a zero mixed gradient, a nearly stationary learning path, and chance-level return. Motivating evidence comes from 22 independent PPO runs in 510K, a four-player card game: the hidden-relation condition produces the shortest behavioral trajectories (0.293 ± 0.049), whereas revealing the same relation increases the path length to 0.328 ± 0.062. We further test the mechanism through continuous information-reveal interventions and direct relation-conditioned gradient measurements while holding rewards and dynamics fixed. These findings expose an overlooked consequence of partial observability: it can remove useful learning signals, not merely increase gradient noise. Thus, shorter trajectories need not indicate more stable learning—they may indicate that the gradients required for learning have been averaged away.
